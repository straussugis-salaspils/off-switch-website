# Why you cannot stop working even when you have no energy left

Someone sits at a laptop late at night.

They are no longer working well: reading one line three times, making simple mistakes and resenting anyone who asks a normal question. The body has said enough.

They understand. They close the laptop.

Then remember one task and open it again.

Not because they missed the general information about rest. They could teach the seminar. Knowledge says, “Stop.” Something inside answers, “You cannot.”

That *cannot* is the useful place to look.

## There is no energy left, but the laptop is open again

From outside, the solution seems obvious: refuse the extra task, delegate, turn off the phone, go to sleep.

Logically, correct.

But if those options felt available, the person would probably have used them. Another intelligent person explaining that night is traditionally reserved for sleep adds little.

Sometimes stopping produces a stronger response than continuing.

Working is already painful. Stopping feels more dangerous.

## “Work less” assumes that choice already exists

The advice assumes two equally available buttons:

```text
continue working
or
stop
```

Internally, the structure may be different.

Continuing is exhausting. Stopping means missing the deadline, losing work or money, disappointing the manager, exposing an error or admitting weakness.

The person is not choosing between work and rest.

**They are choosing between exhaustion and threat.**

While threat feels worse, exhaustion wins by appearing safer.

## Work can briefly switch off fear

Financial risk and responsibility can be real. People have families, loans, employees and obligations. I do not remove responsibility and recommend an enthusiastic collapse of every commitment.

The question is whether the response fits the actual risk.

One person examines the risk and makes a plan. Another imagines saying no and internally sees dismissal, poverty and enforcement officers arriving by lunchtime.

The real risk may be one; the response is six out of five.

Completing the task brings brief relief. Then another task arrives and the fear returns. Work has become the way fear is regulated, one task at a time.

## Error may still mean punishment

For one person, an error is information: correct, learn, continue.

For another it means, automatically, *I am bad; I will be criticised, humiliated, punished or rejected.* The person may be forty-five and have a reasonable manager; the connection may have been learned much earlier.

Then they are not merely trying to do good work. They are preventing an event experienced as danger: check again, redo it personally, do not submit until perfect, do not stop until everything is finished.

Unfortunately, work does not finish. The instruction “complete everything” has been installed in an infinite system—a powerful arrangement for never resting.

## Perfectionism can present fear as a quality standard

Perfectionism looks respectable: reliable, thorough, prepared to stay late.

Some of it may be professionalism. I test whether choice exists.

Can the person decide that this is good enough? Delegate to someone who will do it differently? See a minor error without experiencing a personal verdict? Finish tomorrow what can reasonably wait?

If yes, it may be a quality standard. If no—and fear, guilt and the need to rescue everything start immediately—quality is not the only participant.

## Responsibility does not issue a second body

Projects and teams can genuinely depend on someone. Errors can be costly.

Real responsibility still does not provide another body, eight extra hours and a replacement brain when the first has stopped working.

I separate the task from what attached to it.

Real task: the project needs completing.

Attached response: I alone must save everything; refusal is impossible; asking for resources is shameful; failure means I am bad.

When the two are fused, the whole package appears in the job description even though some arrived from elsewhere.

## Stop treating fear as a work instruction

I do not argue with fear by saying, “Do not worry about losing your job.” We test specific actions.

What happens when the person imagines telling the manager the volume is impossible? Delegating? Ending today with an unfinished list?

Fear, guilt, a picture of dismissal, criticism or the thought *This is not allowed* may appear. That is workable material.

After the response is addressed, we return to the same action. Can the person imagine stopping, assess the risk and make a plan instead of immediately reopening the laptop?

Responsibility remains. Fear stops issuing the only command.

## Then no must appear in the calendar and conversation

Less fear does not teach delegation, a workload conversation or a workable calendar.

Structural work follows. The real planner comes out. We identify priorities, what can move, what can be transferred and which conversation needs to happen.

No must appear in the email, conversation, calendar and task list—occasionally in the decision to stop doing some nonsense inherited from an agreement nobody remembers.

Otherwise the boundary remains a tasteful concept.

## The next overload tests the boundary

Everyone is balanced during a quiet week.

The test is the next large task or impossible deadline. Does the person silently take everything again, or assess volume, name priorities, request resources and refuse part of the load?

If the boundary fails, another lecture on balance is not the answer. Identify what returned: a response or a missing skill. Then test again.

If the person can now stop and the work remains impossible, the next conclusion may be external: too few people, destructive conditions or lost meaning.

# Why thoughts go in circles without reaching a decision

You have considered the problem carefully.

There is a decision. There is a plan. You could explain what you will do tomorrow.

Ten minutes later, the same thought returns. You pick it up, turn it over and reach roughly the same conclusion. Half an hour later, it returns for another inspection.

From the outside, serious intellectual work appears to be under way. Internally, an entire analytical department is holding meetings. The decision does not improve, the plan does not change and nothing moves.

I call this thinking in circles.

## Long thinking is not always useful analysis

My aim is not to stop people thinking. Solving a problem about thought by abolishing thought would be an ambitious piece of management.

Some situations really are complicated. New facts appear. Options need comparing. A decision needs changing or a new action belongs in the plan.

Useful analysis produces something:

- a fact you did not have before;
- a clearer decision;
- an altered plan;
- a concrete next action.

Another process looks equally busy but produces none of these. The content returns, the person experiences it again and the loop closes in the same place.

That may be an automatic emotional response or a well-practised habit of reconsidering everything. Either way, its duration does not make it analysis.

## One question exposes the difference

Put the plan somewhere outside your head: paper, notes app, whatever you will actually open.

When the thought returns, read the plan and ask:

> What has changed?

Not “Am I anxious again?” or “Have I worried about this for long enough?” Ask what changed in the situation.

If there is a new fact, add it. If circumstances changed, change the plan. If you found a better option, excellent—write it down.

If there is no new information and the decision remains the same, you are not continuing to solve the task. You are restarting a familiar loop.

This is a simple question for the logical mind and an inconvenient one for the Department of Meetings About Meetings.

## A loop can face backwards

After a difficult event, a conversation can remain close and vivid. The person revisits what they said, what the other person answered, what they should have said and the precise moment at which they ought to have left.

There may still be a real action: apologise, correct something, ask a question, set a boundary or have the conversation that did not happen.

Put that action in the plan.

But what can no longer be changed does not become changeable during its twenty-fifth internal replay. If the same scene produces the same reaction and no new decision, the past is not being analysed. It is being run through the present once more.

## A loop can also rehearse the future

The forward-facing version usually begins with “What if?”

What if they say no? What if I am late? What if that creates another problem? What if the other problem creates a third, rather imaginative problem?

A useful question about the future produces a branch in the plan:

> If they have not replied by Friday, I will follow up on Monday.

Now there is a condition and an action. Finished.

If the mind then rehearses Friday, Monday, the unanswered message and every possible catastrophe forty more times, the plan is no longer developing. Only the response is.

I sometimes call this the stuck gramophone: “if this, if this, if this” without arriving anywhere.

## A written plan is not a tranquilliser

Writing down a plan does not automatically remove fear. Paper is useful, but it has never claimed to be magic.

The plan preserves the decision so the logical mind does not need to reconstruct the entire case whenever the thought appears. It gives you a reliable test: is there new information or not?

If there is, think.

If there is not, the returning mental and emotional response needs to be addressed as a response. In my work, I teach an Off-Switch practice for interrupting that automatic cycle. The closed technique itself is not reproduced here; the principle matters: keep the useful decision and stop treating every return of the thought as an instruction to decide again.

## “Do not think about it” leaves a vacancy

A person announces, “I will not think about this any more,” then spends half an hour checking whether they are thinking about it.

The loop has acquired a new job title. It has not retired.

Attention needs a direction. Once the automatic cycle is interrupted, what do you want your mind to do now?

- carry out the recorded next step;
- return to the work in front of you;
- prepare for a real conversation;
- rest;
- notice new information when it actually arrives.

The aim is not an empty mind. It is a mind that can be directed.

## Similar complaints can contain different loops

“I cannot stop thinking” is not yet a complete description.

It may be a general habit of reconsidering everything. It may be a past event that still feels present. It may be a future assembled from endless conditional disasters. Or it may contain a genuinely new fact that deserves attention.

I do not give all of these one attractive universal cause. First I examine the structure:

- Is the thought about the past or future?
- What exactly repeats?
- Has a decision already been made?
- Does each repetition change the plan?

Without those distinctions, the advice becomes “think less.” Splendid. We have solved philosophy; implementation remains.

## The result is not perfect silence

Life keeps producing new information, and some decisions deserve more than one round of thought.

The test is more practical. The thought returns. You open the plan. If something changed, you add it and make the next decision. If nothing changed, you recognise the repetition, interrupt the response and return your mind to the direction you chose.

The past stops replaying as though it were happening now. The future stops behaving like a gramophone made entirely of “what if.” A solved problem no longer reopens with a blank file every hour.

This does not prove that no repetitive thought will ever appear again. It proves something more useful: a thought does not have to continue merely because it knows how to return.

Thinking can go back to its proper work—adding facts, changing plans, producing decisions and leading to action.

**And when nothing new has appeared, the meeting may be adjourned.**

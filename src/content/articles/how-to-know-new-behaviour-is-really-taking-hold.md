# How to know whether a new way of acting is really taking hold

A person has decided to act differently. They can explain the decision well and imagine the future situation. In the session, they sound calm and certain.

Everything looks excellent.

I retain one impolite question: what did the person actually do after we spoke?

Deciding, imagining and doing are not the same operation.

**A beautifully imagined future is not yet a constructed one.**

## Mental rehearsal is useful—but not the result

I use future rehearsal to examine a specific forthcoming situation, not to screen a pleasant internal film.

A person imagines having a calm, firm conversation. The other person appears, the opening line begins—and uncertainty returns. The words disappear. Or the urge to soften everything and agree becomes overwhelming.

That is useful information. The imagined scene has revealed an obstacle before the real conversation. We can work with it, rehearse the action again and test what changed.

But even a completely successful rehearsal remains a rehearsal. The conversation has not happened.

Imagined is not done.

## The future scene reveals what is still missing

My first question is simple: imagine doing it; what happens inside?

Anxiety, fear, uncertainty, an unpleasant thought or bodily response may appear. The person may discover they cannot picture the next step at all.

Each obstacle makes the task more precise. Part of an old response may remain. A different one may have appeared. The person may want an action but lack the skill to carry it out. The imagined step may simply be too large.

I do not consider it useful to make somebody repeat a wonderful picture until they learn to give the practitioner the correct answer. If something objects, we need to see it—not paint over it.

## A resource belongs in the action

After an obstacle changes, I may help the person access a needed resource—for example, confidence.

They recall a time when confidence was real, enter that state and then move through the specific future scene from it.

The aim is not to feel good for two minutes in a room. It is to have access to confidence when the door opens, the other person arrives and the sentence must be spoken.

A resource does not replace a skill, create a missing plan or repair impossible conditions. It helps make the chosen action available without the old automatic response taking over.

## The first real test need not be the hardest

Consider fear of lifts. After work with relevant past episodes, we need not begin with the most frightening lift in the person’s history and see whether the Method survives.

In one anonymised piece of work, the first real test began before the lift: a bathroom with a closed door, the light, and the possibility that the door might not open. Once those obstacles no longer controlled the person, the steps moved towards the lift—looking at it, approaching open doors, entering, closing the doors, travelling, and later checking different lifts including the previously difficult one.

This is not a universal exposure instruction for phobias. It illustrates a principle: begin with an accessible real test, then increase difficulty until the person can do the thing they wanted to be able to do.

An accessible step should not become a permanent method for avoiding the actual test.

## Evidence is action in life

A boundary does not exist because its sentence sounded excellent in a session. It exists when the person can set it in a real conversation.

A new way of working exists when the calendar rule changes, an unnecessary task is declined or responsibility is delegated.

The ability to use a lift exists when the person enters and travels—not when they calmly admire the doors in imagination.

Words are valuable information, but actions are the final evidence. The person returns with what happened, giving us precise material for the next decision.

## “It did not work” is data, not a verdict

Reality may not cooperate. The calm conversation becomes silence beside the actual person. The boundary becomes another agreement. The person reaches the lift and does not enter.

That does not automatically prove sabotage, poor motivation or failure of all previous work.

I ask what happened exactly:

- Did the old response return?
- Did a new obstacle appear?
- Was the real situation different from the rehearsal?
- Was the step too large?
- Was a skill or phrase missing?

“It did not work” describes an outcome. The details show what to change.

## Reality edits the next step

We prepare as well as possible. The person goes into life and tries. Then we use the result.

If it worked, the action now exists outside the mind. If it did not, we identify the precise point, change the next step and test again.

Reality is under no obligation to follow our internal screenplay. This is inconvenient and extremely useful. It exposes what preparation missed.

## One successful attempt proves one successful attempt

One lift journey is real progress. It is also one journey in one lift.

Another may be smaller, noisier or close its doors differently. A remaining part of the response may appear there.

New behaviour therefore needs repetition across suitable real situations. There is no magical number. Stability means the behaviour is genuinely available, not that the person performs perfectly or never encounters difficulty.

The decisive sign is that the person can continue without requiring my presence for every attempt.

## Support should increase independence

Early support may be more frequent while I help locate obstacles, select the next level and make sense of unexpected results.

As the person acts independently, meetings can become less frequent and more strategic. They return not for permission to take every step, but with a real result and a specific point that needs adjustment.

Otherwise, we have not built new behaviour. We have built a new dependency on whoever occupies the other square in Zoom.

Change becomes visible in sequence: the old response stops deciding, the person chooses a direction, tests a concrete action, works with the obstacle, acts in life, learns from the result and repeats.

The new way becomes real not because the person declares, “I am different now,” but because they act differently—and can continue to do so on their own.

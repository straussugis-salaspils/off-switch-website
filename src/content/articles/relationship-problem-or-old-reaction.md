# How to distinguish a real relationship problem from an old reaction

Two people are considering divorce.

The reasons may be serious; the relationship may genuinely be over. But during every conversation, one person returns to something that happened fifteen years ago.

The other may have changed since then. A great deal may have occurred in the relationship. Yet the old action still produces enough anger and hurt to become the lens through which all fifteen later years are viewed.

If divorce is needed, divorce is needed. No question.

But I want the person to divorce because of an adequate assessment of what exists now—not because a fifteen-year-old wound has made the decision in advance.

So how can we tell whether the problem belongs to today's relationship or whether the past is making it larger?

## The event was fifteen years ago; the decision is today

An old event can remain important. It may have changed trust, the relationship and the person. I am not suggesting that nothing happened.

But while the memory starts the former anger, fear, hurt or helplessness every time, the present is seen through that response.

Someone may see only who the partner was fifteen years ago and miss what changed. Or they may keep excusing behaviour that remains unacceptable now because they are accustomed to living inside one old story.

A strong emotion does not answer whether to stay or leave. It shows that something catches, but not always what is happening today.

## Removing a response does not erase the action

When the emotional charge reduces, the memory remains.

The person knows what occurred, can describe it and can still judge it wrong. They can still say, “You cannot treat me that way.”

Neutralising a response does not rewrite the biography or convert a bad act into a good one.

If someone no longer cries or becomes furious when remembering, they are not obliged to approve, forgive or continue the relationship. The situation remains what it was; it simply stops producing automatic suffering now.

## Neutrality does not mean tolerating everything

Some people fear losing resentment or anger because they think it will make them permit the behaviour again.

A live boundary and emotional charge are different things.

A boundary can be calm and exact: no, end the conversation, create distance, leave. It does not require boiling internally for a week.

Old reactions can also prevent departure. Fear of being alone, guilt, renewed hope and recurring hurt can keep someone in a place where they have wanted a firm boundary for years.

Emotion is therefore not my guarantee of a good decision. Removing the automatic response does not remove the boundary; often it clarifies it.

## First, I work with the lens through which the present is being viewed

When many difficult events have accumulated, I do not select one convenient episode and declare it the only cause.

The person first learns to stabilize their state. Then they work gradually with accessible memories before approaching more difficult material. Later, we check what still starts the same pattern.

Sometimes old rules also need attention:

- Relationships must be endured at any cost.
- Leaving makes me a bad person.

While such rules carry fear and guilt, the present cannot be assessed freely either.

The aim is not to engineer reconciliation or separation. It is to stop old hurt, fear, anger and inherited rules from deciding on the person's behalf.

## Then the person looks at the same relationship again

After the old response reduces, we return to the same partner, conversation or act.

Sometimes the situation genuinely looks different. The person sees that the partner has changed and that a conversation remains possible.

Sometimes the opposite becomes clear. The old charge is gone, but the behaviour remains unacceptable: lying, pressure, broken agreements or refusal to discuss the problem.

That is also a result.

Emotional work is not required to make a relationship good. It should help reveal what the relationship is.

## Conversation and boundaries create new facts

A calm thought is not enough. The present is tested through action.

The person has a conversation, names what is unacceptable, asks for a specific change and sets a boundary.

The important question is not only whether the wording was elegant. What does the other person do?

Can they listen? Acknowledge the problem? Change the behaviour? Or do they promise, repeat it and apply more pressure when the boundary appears?

These are new facts about today's relationship.

A boundary set only in the mind is still a proposal. It exists in life when it has been expressed and the person can act consistently with it.

## A real problem remains real without intense emotion

Some actions and conditions do not become acceptable after the response changes.

Someone can stop raging and still know that trust is absent, conversations change nothing, or distance is necessary. Hatred is not required for a firm decision.

Sometimes calm leads to staying and renegotiating. Sometimes it produces a much firmer boundary. Sometimes it produces departure.

The Method does not choose one correct ending.

## Running away and leaving calmly feel different inside

When someone runs from an unresolved old life, the argument continues internally. They rehearse, prove and fear recurrence, sometimes bringing the same response into the next relationship.

With a calm decision, the memory remains but does not command the action. The person knows why they are staying or leaving and does not have to prove repeatedly that the other person was bad.

The decision may still be difficult. Divorce, distance and serious conversations are not made easy merely by removing old charge.

The difference is acting on the present rather than trying to end the past through one more decision today.

## The result is neither mandatory reconciliation nor mandatory separation

I do not judge the result by whether the couple remains together.

The result is remembering without the old charge, seeing current behaviour, conducting the conversation, setting a boundary and observing reality's response.

After that, the person may choose acceptance, a new form of relationship, distance or departure.

If divorce is needed, divorce is needed. But the decision should belong to the person today—not to a fifteen-year-old wound.

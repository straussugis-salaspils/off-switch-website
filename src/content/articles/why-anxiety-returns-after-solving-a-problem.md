# Why anxiety can remain after you solve the main problem

For several weeks, someone is anxious about a report.

They think about it in the morning and in the evening. Opening the laptop produces anxiety. Closing it does not help; the report continues working in their head, apparently without respecting office hours.

Eventually, they finish and submit it.

Relief arrives. Sometimes very noticeable relief. They can eat dinner, watch a film and perhaps even resist checking their inbox every seven minutes.

Then, after a while, anxiety becomes noticeable again. The conclusion seems obvious: “Nothing worked. The anxiety came back.”

Before asking why it came back, I would check whether the same thing actually returned.

## The relief was real

Submitting the report ended a real problem. The deadline existed. The work existed. The manager may quite reasonably have expected the result.

One source of pressure disappeared, so the pressure fell. There is no need to explain this away or announce that the report was merely symbolic.

The question is not whether the relief was genuine.

The question is what, exactly, ended with the report. Solving it was useful; it was not required to erase every older response, nearby fear and imagined future at the same time. That would be an ambitious job description for a report.

## One issue may have occupied the foreground

Several responses can contribute to an anxious background at the same time. One of them may move to the front.

The report is currently louder than money, the relationship, the manager and older memories. The person thinks about it most, so it begins to look like the cause of all the anxiety.

But the most visible issue is not necessarily the only one. It may simply occupy the foreground.

> One large event can temporarily drown out everything else.

While the report is dominant, the other responses are harder to notice. When it is submitted, its contribution disappears and the contrast is strong: it was difficult; now it is easier. Later, the other active lines become audible again.

**For something to return, it first has to leave completely.**

## Solving a task and changing an old response are different

Submitting a report, paying a debt or having a difficult conversation solves an external task. That may remove the pressure attached to it.

Changing a specific old response is another operation. In my model, the response is addressed and then tested directly. If that work is complete, I do not describe the old response as having quietly gone away and then returned.

A similar anxiety later may indicate a neighbouring element, another part of the connected cluster, or a new event. Those are different possibilities.

## A calm week is welcome, but it is not a complete test

Someone may feel well for several weeks after the loudest problem ends. That matters. It still does not prove that the whole anxious pattern has changed.

Perhaps no situation activated another part. The manager was away. No unexpected bill arrived. The relationship was calm. Work proceeded normally.

Then a familiar situation appears and the remaining response becomes visible.

That is not necessarily evidence that all earlier work failed. It may finally show what is still active.

## A neighbouring element may remain

Even within one apparent problem, different elements can start the response.

Someone may have changed their reaction to the content of a manager’s criticism while the tone of voice still activates them. Consciously, both are called “fear of my manager.” For the response, they can be separate entry points.

Similar anxiety later does not automatically mean that a completed response has come back to life. A nearby element may have been present all along.

## Sometimes something genuinely new has happened

There is another possibility: the previous work may be complete, and life may produce a new event.

A person is made redundant. A new conflict or loss occurs. Their city enters a war. A new response to a new event is not the return of an old response.

I do not advise treating every anxiety in the present as unfinished childhood. Reality deserves to be checked first.

Sometimes an older background remains. Sometimes another connected element becomes visible. Sometimes something new has happened. These situations require different answers.

## Check what actually changed

“I felt better” and “then I felt anxious again” describe the experience. To find the next step, we need a little more detail.

Which issue ended? What disappeared with it? What remained in the background? Which situation started the response again? Was it a familiar element, a previously unnoticed detail or a new event?

If a situation still starts a response, that is not a reason to declare all the work meaningless. It tells us what to examine next.

## Relief and completion are different claims

Solving one problem can bring genuine relief without ending the whole anxious background.

That does not cancel the relief. It defines its scale.

The useful question is no longer “Why did my anxiety come back?” It is: how do we tell whether a particular anxiety response has actually changed rather than simply becoming quiet for a while?

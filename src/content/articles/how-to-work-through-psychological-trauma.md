# How to work through psychological trauma—and test the result

There is a curious idea that if the pain is severe, the work should begin at its centre—as though depth were measured by how overwhelmed someone became during the meeting.

I work differently.

A person can tell the same story twenty times and react on the twenty-first as though it is happening now.

Telling it may have helped them understand themselves, assemble the facts, stop blaming themselves or name what happened for the first time. All of that matters.

But if the same tone, place or image still removes choice, the work with the response is not complete.

For me, “working through trauma” does not mean erasing memory or finding the officially correct interpretation. It means changing a specific continuing response, testing a similar future and seeing a different action become possible in real life.

## Do not begin with the most difficult memory

If someone cannot return their state to a manageable range independently, I do not begin with causes. First I teach Off-Switch and stabilization.

Learn to brake the car before taking it onto the difficult road.

The person begins with accessible material. We move further only when they can continue without producing another overload. If a new difficult event occurs, the immediate response is stabilized before we return to the earlier sequence.

This is not an examination in courage. A useful pace is one at which the person remains able to do the work—not one that creates another problem through the attempt to solve the first.

## The object of the work needs to be specific

“My childhood” is not a working target. Neither is “all my relationships.” Both have admirable scope and poor handles.

I take one accessible scene: what does the person see, hear and feel, and what meaning does the scene still carry?

I am not trying to reconstruct perfect historical truth. Memory is not a camera. The task is not to prove every detail but to identify the remembered scene that continues to start the response.

Another scene may follow—sometimes logically, sometimes not. The work follows the accessible chain rather than a biography written in advance.

Two opposite mistakes are possible:

- declaring one memory the root of the entire life;
- deciding that every memory must be recovered before anything can change.

I do neither. We work with accessible scenes that genuinely continue to start the response, and after each one we test what remains active.

## Relief during the work does not prove the result

The person imagines the scene and feels calm for the first time. Good.

The result does not need to be protected from examination.

We bring the image back. We add complexity: the words, tone, face or a possible future episode. Then we observe what happens in life.

A specific target counts as changed when:

- the memory no longer starts the old response;
- a similar future does not switch it on automatically;
- the person retains choice in the real situation;
- the facts and necessary action remain visible.

If a reaction appears again, that does not automatically mean the original scene was handled badly. There may be another signal in the same situation, a neighbouring memory, a new real problem or a skill that has not yet been built.

## Removing a reaction does not build a new life automatically

Suppose someone no longer freezes around authority.

That does not mean they automatically know how to negotiate, ask for more money or resign. If the skill was never built, it still needs building.

And if the current manager genuinely harms people, emotional work should not teach someone to tolerate the environment serenely.

After the old response reduces, I ask separately:

- What decision is needed today?
- Which action can the person already take?
- What still needs to be learned?
- What must change in the external situation?

A memory can become neutral while a boundary becomes firmer. There is no contradiction. The person is simply no longer setting the boundary from old helplessness.

## When independent work reaches its limit

After learning the technique, a person may practise independently with relatively accessible reactions.

Severe, multiple or layered events require a different pace—especially when the person easily loses control of the state, events overlap, real danger continues or each step reveals strong new material.

There is no honest universal instruction that says, “Take your worst memory and complete these three steps.” Real safety and present danger always take priority over an emotional exercise.

Working through trauma is not a performance of intensity. It is a sequence that preserves enough stability to reach the result and test it honestly.
